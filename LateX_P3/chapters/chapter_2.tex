This chapter sets out the background the thesis builds on: how emotion is modelled computationally, which encoders are used for each signal, how modalities are combined, what has already been reported on the EAV corpus, and which methodological work bears on evaluating such systems fairly.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Modelling Emotion}
Affective computing, in the sense introduced by Picard \cite{Picard1997AffectiveComputing}, aims to give machines the ability to recognise and respond to human emotion. Two traditions define what is to be recognised. The categorical view, most closely associated with Ekman \cite{Ekman1992Argument}, holds that a small set of basic emotions is shared across cultures, which turns recognition into classification. The dimensional view of Russell \cite{Russell1980Circumplex} instead places emotions in a continuous space of valence and arousal, which captures blends and gradations better but makes the task a regression. EAV labels each trial with one of five categories---Neutral, Anger, Happiness, Sadness and Calmness---so this thesis works in the categorical setting. Its limitation is that ambivalent or mixed states have no label of their own.

A second distinction concerns what each signal measures. Facial and vocal behaviour are produced voluntarily and are shaped by social display rules; a person can smile without being happy. EEG records cortical activity that is far less open to deliberate control. The possibility that the two disagree---that what is shown differs from what is felt---motivated the coherence analysis of this thesis (Chapter~\ref{chap:audit}).

\subsection{Encoders for Each Signal}
Early emotion recognition relied on hand-designed descriptors: texture and gradient histograms for faces; pitch, energy and cepstral coefficients for speech. Learned representations have since replaced them.

\textbf{Vision.} Convolutional networks and, more recently, Vision Transformers \cite{Dosovitskiy2021ViT} dominate facial expression recognition. Lightweight designs such as EfficientFace trade a little accuracy for much lower cost through depth-wise separable convolutions \cite{Zhao2021LightweightFER}; transformer models pre-trained on large facial-emotion collections favour accuracy. The vision encoder here is a ViT already fine-tuned for seven-class facial emotion, re-headed for the five EAV classes.

\textbf{Audio.} Residual networks applied to log-mel spectrograms \cite{He2016ResNet} learn hierarchical acoustic features. The Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST) \cite{Gong2021AST} removes convolution altogether: it splits the spectrogram into patches, treats them as tokens, and is pre-trained on AudioSet's roughly two million clips across 527 sound classes. That pre-training is what lets it adapt to emotional speech from only a few hundred clips per person, and it is the audio encoder used here.

\textbf{EEG.} EEGNet \cite{Lawhern2018EEGNet} is a compact convolutional network for raw multichannel EEG: a temporal convolution, then a depth-wise spatial convolution across electrodes, then a separable convolution. Its small parameter count suits EAV's 280 training trials per participant. An alternative line of work first converts EEG into frequency-band features. Power spectral density over the delta (0.5--4 Hz), theta (4--8 Hz), alpha (8--13 Hz), beta (13--30 Hz) and gamma (30--45 Hz) bands is the classic choice; differential entropy, which for a Gaussian band-limited signal of variance $\sigma^2$ is
\begin{equation}
    h(X) = \tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e\sigma^2),
\end{equation}
has been reported to beat it by 7--19 percentage points on SEED \cite{Zheng2015SEED}. This thesis keeps EEGNet on the raw signal so that the EEG branch matches the EAV reference implementation.

\subsection{Combining Modalities}
Fusion strategies fall into three families.

\textbf{Early fusion} concatenates the modalities' features before a single classifier. It is simple, but it enlarges the input and assumes the modalities are aligned in time; a systematic review by Kalateh et al.\ \cite{Kalateh2024SystematicReview} finds it often trails late fusion for that reason.

\textbf{Late fusion} trains one classifier per modality and combines their outputs, for instance by averaging class probabilities. It tolerates a missing modality and prevents any one signal from dominating training, but it cannot model interactions between modalities. Mean-softmax averaging is the baseline throughout this thesis: it has no parameters, so any learned fusion method that cannot beat it has not justified its complexity.

\textbf{Attention-based fusion} lets a model weight modalities differently for each input. Building on the Transformer \cite{Vaswani2017Attention}, cross-modal attention---one modality's queries attending to another's keys and values---was applied to emotion recognition by Krishna and Patil \cite{KrishnaPatil2020Interspeech} and extended to six directional attention blocks over audio, video and EEG by Lee, Kim and Kim \cite{Lee2024MERCL}. When each modality is summarised by a single vector, as in this thesis, pairwise cross-attention reduces to learned projections, and self-attention over a three-token sequence plays the same role more economically (Section~\ref{sec:fusion_design_rationale}).

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{EEG Alongside Audio-Visual Signals}
Soleymani et al.\ \cite{Soleymani2016EEGFacial} studied continuous emotion recognition from EEG and facial expressions and asked whether EEG's affective content is merely facial-muscle contamination. Their causality analysis found that EEG carries information beyond the facial signal, which is the basic justification for treating EEG as a separate channel. Xing et al.\ \cite{Xing2019EEGAudioVisual} fused differential-entropy EEG features with audio-visual features on DEAP and MAHNOB-HCI and found that the fused system outperformed each single modality, with EEG resolving ambiguity in the external cues. Pan et al.\ \cite{Pan2024OJEMB} combined facial expression, speech and EEG in a single recognition framework. Chen et al.\ \cite{Chen2022EAV} recorded audio, video, EEG and EMG together and reported that fusing all four outperformed bimodal combinations.

\subsection{Fusion Architectures}
Babu et al.\ \cite{Babu2024AffectiveFusionNet} combined Vision Transformers with variational autoencoders and graph-based relational learning in AffectiveFusionNet, showing the value of global context modelling for multimodal recognition. Lee, Kim and Kim \cite{Lee2024MERCL} proposed a three-stage pipeline: per-modality encoders (ViT per frame for video, VGGish for audio, a modified Conformer for EEG, each followed by a residual temporal convolutional network); contrastive pre-training (MERCL) combining within-modality, cross-modality and sample-wise alignment losses; and six-way cross-modal attention for fusion. It reports 83.2\% on DEAP and 90.9\% on SEED, but it has not been evaluated on EAV and its code is not released. Chumachenko, Iosifidis and Gabbouj \cite{Chumachenko2022SelfAttention} introduced self-attention fusion for audio-visual emotion recognition with incomplete inputs, trained with a ``softhard'' modality dropout that randomly removes modalities during training; the modality-dropout head of this thesis adopts that scheme.

\subsection{The EAV Corpus and Prior Results on It}
Lee, Shomanov, Kabidenova and Yazici \cite{Lee2024EAV} released EAV: 42 participants recorded with 30-channel EEG, audio and video in a cue-based conversational task across five emotions. Their per-modality baselines are 36.7\% for audio (SCNN), 52.8\% for vision (DeepFace) and 36.7\% for EEG (EEGNet). The accompanying repository provides data loaders and single-modality trainers but no fusion. In the released data, EEG segments come from the participant's listening turns and audio-visual segments from their speaking turns (Section~\ref{sec:listenspeak_asymmetry}).

Three trimodal fusion methods have since reported results on EAV, all within-subject:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AMERL} (Yin, Shin, Li and Lee \cite{Yin2024AMERL}) applies self-attention fusion over pooled features from modality-specific transformers---close in design to the fusion module here, with stronger encoders and no modality dropout. It reports 70.86\% over 42 participants, and its code is public.
    \item \textbf{Hyper-MML} (Kang et al.\ \cite{Kang2025HyperMML}) replaces pairwise attention with a hypergraph module modelling higher-order relations among the three modalities, reporting 76.65\% accuracy and 76.80\% macro-$F_1$. Its code is not released.
    \item \textbf{EEG-MoCE} \cite{Anon2026EEGMoCE} assigns each modality an expert on a hyperbolic (Lorentz) manifold and learns curvature-aware fusion weights, reporting 75.88\%.
\end{itemize}
None of the three reports a subject-independent evaluation, tests robustness to a missing modality, or states how its reported checkpoint was chosen. Chapter~\ref{chap:within} compares against them.

\section{Methodological Literature}
\label{sec:lit_evaluation}

Four strands of methodological work shape how this thesis evaluates its system.

\textbf{Within-subject versus subject-independent evaluation.} A within-subject protocol trains and tests on data from the same person; a subject-independent one keeps the test person out of training. The first asks how well a system recognises emotion in someone it already knows, the second how well it serves a new user. Because behavioural and physiological signals differ strongly between people, the gap between the two is a property of the data and the representation, and has to be measured.

\textbf{Adapting normalisation statistics.} Li et al.\ \cite{Li2016AdaBN} showed that a network can be adapted to a new domain without labels by re-estimating its batch-normalisation statistics on that domain. The subject calibration of Chapter~\ref{chap:calibration} applies the idea per person: each participant is treated as a domain whose feature statistics are re-estimated from a short unlabelled recording.

\textbf{Selection bias.} Choosing a model, hyperparameter or stopping point on the data later used to report performance inflates the reported figure. Varma and Simon \cite{Varma2006CVBias} quantified the effect for cross-validation, Cawley and Talbot \cite{Cawley2010Overfitting} showed that over-fitting the selection criterion can matter as much as over-fitting the model, and Kriegeskorte et al.\ \cite{Kriegeskorte2009DoubleDipping} documented the same circularity in neuroscience. Chapter~\ref{chap:audit} measures it in the Pre-Thesis~II results.

\textbf{Confidence calibration and attention as explanation.} Modern networks tend to be over-confident, and a single fitted temperature largely corrects this \cite{Guo2017Calibration}; confidence-weighted fusion rules assume such calibration. Attention weights, in turn, show how a model mixes its inputs but are not by themselves an explanation of its decisions \cite{Jain2019AttentionNot}; attention rollout \cite{Abnar2020Rollout} summarises how information flows through stacked layers. Both matter for Chapter~\ref{chap:calibration}.

\section{Expressed and Felt Emotion}
Research on emotion regulation shows that suppressing a visible expression does not remove the underlying response and carries its own physiological cost \cite{Gross1998EmotionRegulation}, and that different measures of emotion---self-report, behaviour and physiology---agree only partly \cite{Mauss2009Measures}. Conditions such as alexithymia, depression and autism are associated with mismatches between how emotion is shown and how it is experienced, which makes the detection of such mismatches clinically attractive.

EAV, however, is a cue-based laboratory corpus: participants are asked to express a given emotion. When their EEG does not match their face and voice, an imperfect performance of the cued emotion explains the mismatch as well as suppression does, and EAV contains no independent record of what participants felt. A mismatch between classifiers adds a third explanation---classification error---which Chapter~\ref{chap:audit} shows accounts for the patterns found here. No clinical claim is made.
